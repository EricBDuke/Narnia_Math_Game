PHASE 1 - DISCOVER

THE DISCOVERY CALL

A discovery call is exactly what the name suggests: A general introduction and a chance for you as the designer and your prospective client to start to get to know each other. The goal of this discovery call is to get a sense for whether there’s potential for a more serious, longer-term working relationship.

Allocate 15-30 minutes for this call. Always give yourself a little buffer before and after the discovery call to prepare and follow up. Review their client intake email/message. Note any keywords that identify their emotions or how they feel about their space (i.e. embarrassed, overwhelmed, frustrated, etc.).

Keep in mind that this call is your sales tool, and you will need a strategy if you want it to work. People tend to want ideas and will take any free advice or ideas offered.

It is your job to protect yourself, and focus the initial call as a discovery opportunity to give you a chance to know if this is going to be a good fit. Your time is valuable, make sure the project is realistic, and you aren’t just being used to offer ideas for free.

THE FIRST FOLLOW-UP EMAIL

After the discovery call, follow up with your potential client, sharing notes from the call and schedule in a time for an in-person consultation if they wish to proceed. For all your email correspondences, consider adding a memorable signature with your logo or image as well as links to your work and social accounts.

Encourage the client to put together a folder of images that they are drawn to so you can go through them together in person, and explain that the consultation fee will be taken off the first bill. That way if you are hired, they pay for your time and initial ideas, and if you are not hired, you won’t give away your ideas for free. If the client is a referral from a colleague or friend, then maybe you can wave this initial fee.

THE CONSULTATION

A consultation meeting is a paid service either in-person or via a video call and is usually around 60-90 minutes. You as the interior designer are selling your expertise and insight, usually at a fixed fee. A consultation must provide value in and of itself. You need to be ready to deliver an experience that’s worth paying for and treat it like a job interview.

This is where you can share your new client questionnaire, which is a list of questions to help get to know a client’s needs and expectations (more on this below). It’s similar to an intake survey you would fill out if you were visiting a doctor or personal coach for the first time, and these questions can help you define a potential client.

During the initial consultation, ask the client to show you the images they collected before the visit and look through these together.

Most people are not great at verbally describing such things, plus what you see in your mind’s eye might be quite different from the image they’re envisaging. So looking at a photograph together can be a beneficial experience, often setting up a meaningful conversation. This is where you can ask them what they like and what they do not like.

Also ask them what their interests are, what their color preferences are (make sure you have some color decks from paint companies with you, so both you and the client are seeing, for instance, the same “light blue”), whether they like entertaining at home, whether they have overnight guests, how they see the house working (and not working), which rooms they’d like changed and so on.

Explain that you’re not being nosy; you’re trying to start putting together the foundations of the eventual design scheme.

Also figure out what exactly your client’s needs are while you are on site. It is very important to get to know your client and their goals.

Your goal is your client’s goal and a smooth process without any discrepancy. Make sure you and your client are on the same page before your start the design process.

THINGS TO BRING TO THE CONSULTATION

Below are some optional items to bring to the first consultation:

  • Notepad and pen or laptop/tablet to take notes
  • Measuring tape
  • Camera or smartphone to take pictures of the space
  • Your portfolio
  • Testimonials
  • Folder of images showing different design styles
  • Paint and fabric swatches
  • Business cards

QUESTIONS TO ASK DURING THE CONSULTATION

Note that some of these questions can be asked during the initial discovery call.

WHAT IS YOUR PROJECT?

This should be your conversation opener. Your client must see that you are willing to work closely with them - and the best way to do that is to inquire about their needs and expectations. Discuss feedback during the initial consultation and talk about potential solutions to their problems.

WHAT IS YOUR BUDGET?

When creating the client questionnaire, interior designers must also inquire about the budget. Some people will answer this question right away, whereas others may feel uncertain and awkward about their budget.

WHERE DID YOU HEAR ABOUT MY SERVICES?

Every detail matters, so a new client questionnaire should also include how they found out about the project. Did they use the Internet to find you, or did someone else refer them to you? Make a note to thank your previous interior design clients for referring them to you.

WHAT ARE YOUR CONTACT DETAILS?

You may need to contact your potential clients for other projects, which is why your interior design client questionnaire should also include their home address, email, and phone number.

DO YOU HAVE SOME INSPIRATIONAL IMAGES THAT CAN REPRESENT YOUR IDEAS?

Words are a good way to gather information, but in order to truly get the client’s vision, you should also get them to show you some pictures of what they want. If they are using Google forms to complete the online questionnaire, then you may ask them to submit the design ideas there.

ARE THERE ANY POTENTIAL CHALLENGES TO THIS INTERIOR DESIGN PROJECT?

Perhaps the client lives on a rented property, which means the interior designer may not have much liberty in the aspects that could be modified. Or maybe the project has to be done in a short time. Either way, these things must be discussed beforehand.

WHAT DESIGN STYLES DO YOU LIKE OR DISLIKE?

Your interior design questionnaire should also include styles that they may or may not like. Perhaps they have a potential color palette that they like or may absolutely dislike. This can give you a better visual of the interior design style that they are looking for.

HAVE YOU WORKED WITH OTHER INTERIOR DESIGNERS BEFORE?

This question can give you a lot of relevant feedback in regard to your potential client. For instance, let’s say that they worked with someone before who didn’t succeed in meeting their expectations. Or perhaps they really liked what they did before and are looking for something similar.

IS THERE ANYTHING YOU’D LIKE TO KEEP?

Once more, this is a very important question that you should not skip. For instance, let’s say that your client has a tiger-print rug that they really want to keep. In that case, the interior decor must have a similar style. However, if it doesn’t fit the design that they are after, it’s your job as an interior designer to come up with alternatives.

WHO WILL BE LIVING IN THE HOUSE?

Depending on who will live in the house, you may want to create your project around it. For instance, you may have to design a room for a 30-year-old adult and one for a 6-year-old girl. Needless to say, they will want different things, so you must adapt your design around their preferences. Ask if there are any pets in the house. This could have a bearing on your choice of finishes. Also ask if elderly people come to stay or is it likely that elderly people will move in at some stage? If so, you will need to consider non-slip flooring and perhaps increased lighting in some areas.

DO YOU NEED EXTRA STORAGE?

Ask if there is adequate storage throughout the house. Is it enough for future needs? Does the client need extra rooms in the house? Can they be created from attic or basement space? Are there any rooms the client doesn’t use? Are there any areas they would like to be bigger? Do they need a walk in wardrobe or large pantry?

WHEN WOULD YOU LIKE THIS PROJECT TO BE COMPLETED?

Depending on your client’s current design, you may require some extra time in order to create your own model. Moreover, if their time is limited, you also need to know the frame so that you may create a feasible project. Take the client’s time in mind as well. For instance, if they need to be at home or at least in the vicinity during that time, it can significantly affect your process. You may not be able to do much work if they are around.

THE SECOND FOLLOW-UP EMAIL

Once you’ve had a consultation with a client, it’s essential to solidify the ideas discussed and provide a clear plan of action.

To ensure everyone is on the same page, it’s important to send a follow-up email summarizing your notes and confirming the details of the conversation.

In this email, you can emphasize your commitment to helping the client by offering to develop design ideas tailored to their needs.

One effective way to convey these ideas is through a mood board, which visually communicates the overall aesthetic and concept. You can propose including a mood board as part of your services and ask if the client would like to proceed with this step.

Consider attaching an invoice for the mood board to this email, demonstrating your professionalism and preparedness. However, it’s also acceptable to wait for the client’s confirmation before sending the invoice, giving them the opportunity to express their interest in moving forward.

By taking these steps, you demonstrate your dedication to providing a comprehensive interior design service and establish a clear path forward with your client.
