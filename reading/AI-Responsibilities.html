PROFESSIONAL, ETHICAL AND LEGAL RESPONSIBILITIES WHEN USING AI IN INTERIOR DESIGN

The use of artificial intelligence in interior design introduces opportunities to work more efficiently, explore ideas rapidly and communicate design concepts in new ways. However, the use of AI also creates professional, ethical and legal responsibilities.

Interior designers remain responsible for the work they produce, regardless of whether AI has been used to assist in its development. AI should therefore be treated as a design tool rather than as an authority or independent decision-maker.

Designers need to consider not only what AI can do, but also whether it is appropriate to use it, what information can safely be provided to it, how its outputs should be evaluated, and how those outputs are communicated to clients and other stakeholders.

PROFESSIONAL RESPONSIBILITY AND ACCOUNTABILITY

Using AI does not transfer professional responsibility from the designer to the technology.

An AI system might suggest a spatial arrangement, material, construction method or accessibility solution. However, the designer remains responsible for determining whether that suggestion is appropriate for the project.

This is particularly important when design decisions affect:

  • Health and safety;
  • Accessibility and inclusive design;
  • Fire and life safety;
  • Compliance with relevant legislation, codes and standards;
  • Material performance;
  • Ergonomics;
  • Construction feasibility;
  • Environmental performance; and
  • The wellbeing of occupants.

For example, an AI system might generate a visually convincing restaurant interior containing circulation paths that are too narrow, inappropriate furniture clearances or an unsafe relationship between occupants and an exit.

The visual realism of the image does not make the proposal technically correct.

A useful principle for designers is:

AI may assist with a decision, but it does not assume responsibility for the decision.

The designer must retain professional oversight.

ACCURACY, MISINFORMATION AND AI HALLUCINATIONS

Generative AI systems can produce information that sounds authoritative while being inaccurate, incomplete or entirely fabricated. This behaviour is commonly described as an AI hallucination.

For an interior designer, this creates particular risks when researching technical information.

An AI system might incorrectly provide:

  • Dimensions or minimum clearances;
  • Requirements supposedly contained within a building code;
  • Accessibility requirements;
  • Fire ratings;
  • Acoustic performance data;
  • Sustainability credentials;
  • Product specifications;
  • Manufacturer information;
  • Costs or lead times; or
  • References to standards that do not exist.

Consequently, AI should not be treated as the final source of technical or regulatory information.

Designers should return to the authoritative source.

Depending on the information being investigated, this may include legislation, building codes, Local building standard, applicable standards, government guidance, manufacturer technical data, certification documentation, consultant advice and project-specific contractual information.

An important habit for students to develop is therefore:

Do not ask only, “Does this answer sound convincing?”

Ask, “How can I verify that this is true?”

CLIENT CONFIDENTIALITY AND DATA PRIVACY

Interior designers regularly work with information that may be confidential or commercially sensitive.

This can include:

  • Client names and contact information;
  • Residential addresses;
  • Architectural drawings;
  • Photographs of private residences;
  • Floor plans;
  • Security information;
  • Project budgets;
  • Tender information;
  • Commercially sensitive workplace information;
  • Unpublished designs; and
  • Information about occupants or employees.

Entering information into an external AI system may mean that project information is being provided to a third-party technology provider.

Before uploading project information, designers should understand the AI platform being used, their organisation’s policies, contractual obligations to the client and the platform’s current privacy and data-handling arrangements.

A useful question to ask before uploading information is:

“Would I be comfortable sending this information to an external organization?”

If the answer is no, it may not be appropriate to enter the information into an external AI system without suitable approval and safeguards.

Where AI is permitted, identifying or confidential information may sometimes need to be removed or anonymized before the tool is used.

COPYRIGHT AND INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY

Generative AI creates complex questions surrounding copyright and intellectual property.

Interior designers should consider both the information supplied to an AI system and the material produced by it.

For example, a designer may wish to upload photographs, drawings, artwork or another designer’s work and ask an AI system to generate something similar. The fact that material is available online does not necessarily mean that it can be used without restriction.

Designers should also be cautious about prompts that deliberately seek to reproduce another designer’s distinctive work.

For example:

“Create a hotel lobby exactly in the style of [living designer or design practice].”

A more professionally appropriate approach is to identify the underlying design characteristics being investigated:

“Explore a hotel lobby using restrained materiality, strong geometric forms, indirect lighting and a limited neutral palette.”

This encourages the designer to investigate design principles rather than imitate a particular designer’s work.

Students should also recognise that questions surrounding ownership and copyright of AI-generated material can vary according to jurisdiction, the particular AI service and the circumstances in which the work was produced.

Current professional or legal guidance should therefore be consulted where ownership is important.

TRANSPARENCY AND DISCLOSURE

AI can produce highly realistic images. This creates an ethical responsibility to communicate clearly what an image represents.

Consider a client being shown a photorealistic image of a proposed interior.

The client may reasonably assume that:

  • The furniture shown has been selected;
  • The materials are available;
  • The lighting has been designed;
  • The dimensions are accurate;
  • The construction is feasible; and
  • The image represents what will eventually be built.

None of these assumptions may be true if the image was generated during early AI-assisted concept exploration.

Designers should therefore consider whether AI-generated material needs to be identified as such and, more importantly, clearly communicate the status and purpose of the image.

For example:

AI-assisted concept image – indicative only. Materials, furniture, dimensions and construction details are subject to design development and verification.

Transparency helps prevent exploratory imagery from being mistaken for resolved design documentation.

BIAS, REPRESENTATION AND INCLUSIVE DESIGN

AI systems learn patterns from large collections of existing information and imagery. These sources can contain cultural, social and demographic biases.

Those biases may subsequently appear in AI-generated design imagery.

For example, asking an AI system to generate images of a “luxury homeowner”, “corporate executive”, “construction worker”, “family”, “older person” or “person with a disability” may produce stereotypical representations.

Bias can also influence the environments AI generates.

An AI-generated workplace may unconsciously assume particular physical abilities, cultural behaviours, family structures or ways of working.

Interior designers should therefore critically examine:

  • Who is represented?
  • Who is absent?
  • What assumptions are being made about users?
  • Does the design assume that all users have similar physical abilities?
  • Are cultural references being used appropriately?
  • Does the imagery reinforce stereotypes?
  • Would the proposed environment genuinely accommodate diverse users?

Inclusive design requires human understanding, consultation and judgement. Generating an image containing diverse occupants is not the same as designing an inclusive environment.

CULTURAL APPROPRIATION AND CULTURAL CONTEXT

Interior design frequently draws upon cultural histories, traditions, craft, motifs and materials. AI makes it extremely easy to combine cultural references without understanding their meaning.

A designer could, for example, ask AI to create an interior that is “inspired by Indigenous culture” and receive visually compelling imagery containing patterns or symbols detached from their cultural significance. This creates the risk of reducing cultural identity to an aesthetic style.

Designers should investigate the origin and meaning of cultural references and consider whether consultation, permission or collaboration is appropriate.

AI-generated imagery should not replace engagement with communities, cultural knowledge holders, artists, craftspeople or other relevant specialists.

The question should not simply be: “Can AI generate this aesthetic?”

It should also be: “Is it appropriate for us to use this cultural reference, and who should be involved in that decision?”

FABRICATED PRODUCTS AND MATERIALS

One particularly important risk for interior designers is the ability of generative AI to create convincing objects that do not exist.

An AI-generated interior might contain a chair that resembles a commercial product but has never been manufactured. It may generate fictional lighting fixtures, impossible material finishes or products that appear structurally plausible but could not actually be constructed. This becomes problematic if a client interprets these objects as products that can be purchased or specified.

Designers must therefore distinguish between:

conceptual objects – imagery used to communicate a general design direction; and specified products – actual products whose manufacturer, dimensions, performance, availability and cost have been verified.

AI-generated imagery should not be treated as a product specification.
