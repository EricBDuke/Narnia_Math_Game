PHASE 2 - PLAN

DESIGN CONCEPT/MOOD BOARD

Using the information obtained during the Discovery Phase, you can analyse the client’s needs and wants and develop a design concept.

At this stage, you should brainstorm and develop ideas gathered during the analysis stage. You should explore several alternatives to ensure you cover all aspects of the design brief, and later, you may want to combine several of these ideas for the final concept.

Once you have decided on the design direction, you will need to source a broad range of images that communicate your design ideas, and these can include any of the following:

  • Inspirational images to reflect the emotive feeling of the design
  • Decorative features and elements
  • Furniture, art and accessories
  • Colors
  • Materials
  • Images of complete rooms

Gathering the images will help you finalize the design concept, and you can create a shortlist of these to use on the mood board for the concept presentation.

The mood board is a tool we use to show the client your interpretation of the brief and for you to communicate the overall look and feel of your design ideas. It is a handy tool to initiate further discussion on the design and to confirm the client’s decorative preferences and style.

FEE STRUCTURE AND QUOTATION

Once you understand what the project will entail, you can decide on the type of fee you wish to use and create a formal quotation to outline the financial components of the project.

You are to present the quotation to the client at the upcoming concept presentation meeting to outline your role in the project and the fee you will charge if they choose to hire you for the project. Later, if they agree to hire you, the quotation will form the basis of the formal contract and agreements.

DESIGN CONCEPT PRESENTATION

The second meeting is an opportunity to present your design ideas to the client and to see if they want to hire you. In its simplest form, think of it like a sales pitch in which you are trying to sell your design concept to the client.

If the client likes your design concept, you are in a great position as you can progress with the project, present your quotation, and discuss the budget and project details.

But what happens if they don’t like what you proposed? Don’t be surprised. That’s not at all uncommon, nor is it necessarily a bad thing.

Think of your first scheme as a starting point and return to the drawing board. The most important thing to take from this situation is that you have clarified the client’s style or design direction, which may not have been clearly communicated to you previously.

You may re-work your ideas free of charge or for a nominal fee until the client is satisfied and accepts the design concept.

DESIGN BRIEF (SCOPE OF WORK)

In this phase, we take the information gathered from the initial meeting and use it to develop the design brief, also known as the scope of work (SOW).

The scope of work helps in preventing miscommunication and waste management by documenting the needs, processes, and execution for the particular project you are working on.

A well thought out SOW helps you and your team (if applicable) focus on project goals, understand milestones, improve success rates, and contribute to effortless communication. It also enables effective work delegation and ensures that you allot tasks to people with the requisite expertise.

Depending on the complexity of the project, you may find that you have gathered an overwhelming amount of information, resulting in not knowing where to start. It is at this point that you should distil the information you have gathered and organize it into a usable format.

Creating the brief can be a little overwhelming as there is a lot of information to cover. It can be broken down into three main points:

  • What are the job requirements?

  • What options exist?
  • What action is required to complete the analysis?

This document will change as the project progresses, but it is an ideal way of keeping track of the job.

Ensure the document lays down the project goals and relevant design elements for the particular project. Include features such as reports, procurement projections, milestones, deliverables, pricing, end products, time frame, and the scope of services that are to be provided to the client.

These are also shared with the client so that they know the purported delivery deadline and how the interior design project will move forward.

CONTRACT

Once you have confirmation to proceed with the project, it’s important to write out all the goals and statements in an agreement. That agreement will be a guidance for your client and yourself.

It includes a description of the design process, how much work and hours you will apply to that job and your fee. This needs to be agreed and signed by your client, before your design process starts.

You can outline the terms of the agreement in a “Letter of Acceptance” accompanied by a formal contract agreement. For minor works, for example, a room redesign, only a “Letter of Acceptance” would be required.

SITE EVALUATION

Once the contract has been signed by both parties, you can then take a deposit and get to work by conducting a thorough site evaluation.

Conducting a site evaluation of the rooms to be decorated and the building overall is an integral part of the analysis stage. It is better not to trust your memory in these situations; instead, photograph and write notes on everything that could impact on the budget or design.

The site evaluation should be conducted with the client present so that you can ask them questions about your observations and prepare them for possible costs that will be incurred from trades and services.

Things to consider during the site evaluation:

PHOTO AND VIDEO

Be sure to take a lot of photographs of the room and zoom in on any details that may be of importance. It is good practice to stand in the center of the room and photograph each wall to ensure the entire project has been captured. Walkthrough videos with key talking points (these can be filmed on your phone) will help if you ever need to refer back to the project.

FLOOR PLAN

If the client does not have a floor plan of the house, you will need to measure the rooms and sketch a floor plan on site.

Be sure to include the ceiling height, size and location of all doors, windows and fixed elements. If you have a compass note on the floor, plan the orientation of the home.

SITE EVALUATION REPORT

We have prepared a checklist for you to use to help conduct the site evaluation. You can download a copy of the site evaluation template provided at the end of the module or you can create your own report based on the following observations:

  • Have a good look at ceilings, floor, walls, woodwork and windows. What sort of condition are they in?
  • Do the fixed elements like cupboards, shower, sanitary ware, sinks and cabinetry need to be replaced?
  • How’s the wiring? Has the switchboard been updated with safety switches? Do you think it will need replacing?
  • Note the position of power points. Will you need to add extra?
  • Is the lighting adequate? Is it in the correct location? Does it suit the style of the home?
  • Is there enough natural lighting in the house? Do you think it would benefit from extra windows or skylights? Are the existing windows big enough?
  • What’s the state of the floor? Is it in good condition? Would the owners like to change it? Are there floorboards under the existing flooring?

DESIGN DEVELOPMENT

After you have completed the evaluation, you can start developing your design ideas for the project. This can include:

  • Inspirational images to reflect what the design will look like
  • A mood board of every room you’re designing so you can picture the end result
  • Confirmation on the color palette, textures and patterns you’ll use throughout
  • A floor plan (to-scale) of each room with all proposed furniture mapped out
  • Detailed explanation of changes you’ll be making to each room
  • A list of trades you’ll be using if you need to engage any (paint, curtains, etc.)
  • A few design curveballs that you’ve come up with

During this phase, communicate with your client and ask for feedback every step of the way. In an ideal world, the client would love every single idea, but there might be a few things they’re unsure about. Keep in mind that developing a design is highly labor-intensive. This step of the interior design process can last between 4 to 12 weeks depending on the size of your project. This time is totally necessary and the results will be well worth the wait.

DESIGN PRESENTATION

Here, you’ll present to your client and share the drawings, furniture selections and furnishes, along with cost estimates. It’s important that you present the final design in a clear and tangible way, so there are no misunderstandings between you and the client.

If required, you’ll then adjust the design, based on the feedback from your client before moving onto the final design.

Through every step of the design process, it is important to present your work in a professional manner, but even more so for the final design because you are likely to leave a copy of your presentation with the client to keep.
